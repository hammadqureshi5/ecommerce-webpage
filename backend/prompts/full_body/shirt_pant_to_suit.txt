The first image is the person. The second image is the garment reference.

Put the suit from the second image onto the person in the first. The second
image is the only authority for the clothes: their colour, their cloth, their
cut, and every piece and detail worn with them. The first image is the only
authority for the person, the pose, the framing, the light and the background,
and it says nothing about what the clothes should look like — not their colour,
not their cut, and not the length of their sleeves.

Build every piece the reference wears. A two-piece reference gives a jacket and
trousers. A three-piece reference gives a jacket, trousers and a waistcoat: a
further piece in the same cloth, buttoned up the centre with its own row of
buttons and a V opening at the chest, filling the space between the open jacket
fronts so that the shirt shows only above it and beside it. Count the pieces in
the reference and build that many.

The person's own shirt and pants go completely. What shows at the neckline and
down the front opening is whatever the reference wears under its jacket, and
nothing of the casual outfit underneath stays visible.

Colour. Take the suit's colour from the reference and hold it: the same hue, the
same depth, the same saturation. Read which way the hue leans, warm or cool,
toward one neighbour or the other, and keep it exactly where it sits rather than
at the colour next to it. Do not settle it toward the charcoal, navy and mid
grey that business suits are usually made in. Do not carry over the colour of
whatever the person is wearing in the first image. Do not retune it to the light
or the background.

One cloth. The jacket, the trousers and any waistcoat are cut from a single
length of cloth: the same colour, the same weave, the same sheen, top to bottom.
They are never two different colours and never two different materials.

Pattern. The cloth is plain unless the reference shows otherwise, and plain
cloth is smooth and unbroken, one flat colour from shoulder to hem. Draw a
check, a windowpane, a pinstripe or a herringbone only when that pattern is
visible in the reference itself, and then at its scale and its spacing.

Cut and structure. A soft, unstructured, casual suit stays soft: a natural
shoulder with little padding, a relaxed lapel roll, matte cloth and easy folds
through the body. A formal structured suit stays structured: defined shoulders,
a flat lapel roll, crisp cloth and few sharp folds. Match the lapel style and
width, the button stance and the number of buttons, the jacket length and the
vent. A jacket worn open in the reference stays open here, falling away from the
body by the same amount; a jacket fastened in the reference is fastened here, on
the same button.

Sleeves come from the reference, never from the person. Find where the jacket
sleeve ends on the arm in the second image and end it there. If the reference
sleeve reaches the wrist, the finished sleeve reaches the wrist and covers the
whole forearm, even where that forearm is bare in the first image: bare skin in
the first image is never a reason to shorten a sleeve, and the arm is repainted
with cloth over it. If the reference sleeve ends above the elbow, it ends above
the elbow. What the person is wearing now has no say in this.

Under the jacket. Look at the neckline in the reference and build what is
actually there. If a collar sits against the neck, the finished layer is a
collared dress shirt: a stiffened collar standing at the throat with its points
spread across the chest, a buttoned placket running down the centre front, and a
band of the same cloth showing at each wrist below the jacket sleeve. If instead
the neckline is a plain knit edge meeting the skin with nothing rising from it,
the finished layer is a soft knit t-shirt, its opening cut to the same depth on
the chest as the reference cuts it, whether that is a round crew, a V or a wide
scoop. Its colour holds either way: a white inner layer stays white and never
drifts to grey, cream, beige or tan, and any other colour holds its own hue and
lightness just as firmly.

The tie is the detail most easily missed, so look for it deliberately. Find the
narrow strip of cloth running from between the collar points down the centre of
the chest, and check it before deciding there is none. A tie is very often close
in colour to the shirt behind it — silver, pale grey, cream, ivory, even white —
and a tie that barely separates from the shirt is still a tie. If that strip is
there, it appears here: a knot filling the opening between the collar points,
widening into a blade that runs down the shirt front until the waistcoat or the
fastened jacket covers it, in the reference's own colour, width and weave,
including a fine dot, stripe or check that only shows up close. Only when the
shirt front is plainly bare from collar to waist is there no tie to draw.

Pocket square. If cloth shows at the chest pocket, draw it: a flat fold or a
loose puff standing above the pocket edge, in the reference's colour and
pattern, showing the same amount above the edge. Where the chest pocket is
empty, it stays empty, and no boutonniere, lapel pin, scarf or other accessory
appears that the reference does not show.

Waist. Suit trousers close with a flat tailored waistband: a firm band of the
same cloth, sitting smooth against the body, with a concealed hook or a single
button and a plain fly. Nothing gathers it and nothing is tied at the front.
Draw belt loops and a belt only when the reference plainly shows them, matching
their width, colour and spacing. Where the jacket or the waistcoat covers the
waist in the reference, let it cover the waist here too rather than inventing a
closure to fill the gap.

Pockets. Draw only the pockets the reference shows: on the jacket a welt pocket
at the chest and two hip pockets, jetted or flapped, lying flat against the
cloth; on the trousers at most a slanted side pocket and a welt pocket at the
back. The outside of each thigh is plain cloth from hip to knee, with nothing
applied or stitched onto it.

Trousers. Match the rise, the front crease, the leg width and taper, and the
point where the hem falls over the shoe.

Keep the person's face, hair, skin tone, body shape, hands, pose, footwear,
background, lighting and camera angle exactly as they are.
